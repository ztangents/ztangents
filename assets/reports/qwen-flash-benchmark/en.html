Benchmark report · 2 November 2026

Qwen 3.8 Flash ×3 vs GPT-6.1 Sol vs GPT-6.1 Terra

Five coding-agent setups on Godot game-development tasks and creative web builds. Sol is the strongest overall. Terra, a different model in Sol's harness, matches Sol on Godot and is the fastest, but its creative pages score well below Sol's. Three Qwen 3.8 Flash deployments trail both on Godot and are level with each other there: IQ4_XS and IQ3_S on one 16 GB workstation GPU, and NVFP4 on two RTX 6000 Pro GPUs under SGLang. NVFP4 is three to seven times faster than the workstation builds, but its creative pages lose to both of them side by side.

What was compared

The five setups

Each side is a full stack: model, serving and agent harness together. The three Qwen setups are the same model under the same harness and settings; they differ in quantization, server and hardware. Sol and Terra share a harness and settings, so the difference between them is the model alone. All five ran in the same Ubuntu 24.04 arm64 VM, inside GameDevBench's strict sandbox. The agent sees a private home folder and the task folder, and its network reaches only its own model endpoint.

Godot 4.4.1 · 58 scored tasks · 600 s per task

GameDevBench

Each GameDevBench task hands the agent a Godot project and a change to make; an automated check then decides pass or fail. We ran a seeded sample of 60 of its 333 tasks with the leaderboard's settings: strict sandbox, runtime video on, no MCP, 600 seconds per task. Two sampled tasks are excluded because their reference solutions also fail in this VM, leaving 58.

Web games, SVG and simulations · 40 tasks · judged blind

ArtifactsBench

ArtifactsBench asks for a working web page from a short prompt. We drew a seeded 40-task sample from its 1,825 tasks, weighted toward games: 24 games across all nine game subgenres, 6 SVG drawings, 4 web visuals and 6 physics or 3D simulations. Each agent built one self-contained index.html per prompt in the same sandbox, with 1,200 seconds per task.

Each page was screenshotted three times in Chromium. Claude Opus 5.5 judges then scored two answers to a task side by side, as anonymous A and B, against the task's official 10-item checklist of 10 points each. Like the official judge, they read the full source and the screenshots. We ran one blind round for each of the ten pairs of setups, so each page has one judgement against each of its four rivals. A setup's score on a task is the mean of those four.

Four tasks side by side

First screen of each page, two seconds after load. Each score is the mean of the page's four judgements.

Earth-Moon orbits

Task 394 · physics simulation · medium

Sol's page is the most polished, with lit, textured bodies, a Moon phase card and a timeline. The normal IQ4 build comes second with the strongest physics: a barycentric two-body model on a true Keplerian ellipse, with zoom, pan, trails and a live distance readout, though it leaves out the Sun. IQ3's physics is just as real, but its default speed runs about 1,200 days a second, so the Moon smears into a web of trails. Terra's clean dashboard has a correct Moon phase panel, but its orbits are simple squashed circles. NVFP4 finished without saving a page.

SIM card in yellow and black

Task 46 · SVG image · easy

Sol and Terra lead with realistic proportions and convincing contact chips. IQ3's card has a clean chamfer and working flip and download buttons, but its header text runs off the card. The normal IQ4 build drew a stubby portrait card with a shine sweep and pointer tilt, but its chip is a stylised frame rather than a contact pad. NVFP4 put an HTML element inside the SVG text, so the chip and the text never render as graphics and spill out as a line of plain text below the card.

Racing game

Task 1013 · sports game · medium

Every Qwen build made a real racer here, and all three outscored Terra. IQ4 wrote an OutRun-style pseudo-3D engine with curves, hills, fog, traffic and an attract mode, and IQ3 a similar one with roadside scenery and engine sound. NVFP4 built a top-down circuit racer with a countdown, three laps, AI rivals and a minimap. Sol's pseudo-3D race adds laps, a race clock and a starting grid. Terra's coastal time attack drives well, with drift and traffic, but has no laps, opponents or countdown.

Prize wheel with editable odds

Task 1202 · casual game · easy

All five wheels work. IQ4's is the most complete Qwen page, with slices sized by weight, an on/off switch per prize and a Monte Carlo fairness tester. IQ3's draws weighted slices with sound and confetti. NVFP4's works, but its whole interface is in Chinese although the prompt was in English. Terra's slices are all the same size, so the wheel does not show the odds. Sol's wheel matches its odds table, with eased motion.

      Read the numbers with these in mind

      Method and caveats

      • Effort is not matched. The Qwen builds ran at medium, Sol and Terra at xhigh. On the Godot tasks, the IQ4 build's highest setting only added timeouts. On the creative tasks the Qwen builds rarely hit the limit, so a higher-effort Qwen run there is the obvious follow-up.
      • The three Qwen deployments differ in more than quantization. IQ4_XS and IQ3_S ran as GGUF files on the Strata server on one workstation GPU; NVFP4 ran on SGLang across two RTX 6000 Pro GPUs. The servers may apply different default sampling settings, which we did not control, so NVFP4's lower creative scores could come partly from serving rather than from the quantization alone.
      • Time was not measured under equal conditions. Every Qwen build ran one task at a time on its own server. Sol and Terra used OpenAI's service, four Godot tasks or two creative tasks at once, and their times also depend on that service's load. The first NVFP4 attempt was discarded after its server stopped answering four minutes in; the rerun had no connection errors.
      • The creative judge is Claude Opus 5.5, not the official Gemini 2.5 Pro. The scores compare these setups with each other and cannot be placed on the ArtifactsBench leaderboard.
      • Round-robin judging. Fourteen blind rounds, with fresh judges and the same brief and checklists: one for each pair of the five setups, and four more for the abliterated IQ4 build. Judges score a page about 2 to 3 points lower when the other answer is Sol's; every setup meets Sol once, so the mean of its judgements evens this out. Sol's five rounds agree closely, from 76.1 to 77.8.
      • Long pages are cut at 108,000 characters for judging, matching the official 27k-token limit. Embedded base64 fonts and images are replaced by a placeholder first. Sol's task 380 page is 4 MB, almost all fonts; in the first round the judge saw almost none of its code, so that task was rejudged with the fonts elided. Even then its scripts fall past the cut, so in every round it was judged on its markup, styles and screenshots. The abliterated IQ4 answer moved 7 points between those two judges, a rough measure of judge noise.
      • Blind judging. Answers were labelled A and B at random, the key was kept outside the judges' folders, and the pages were checked for any text naming a model or tool. A few judges listed or hashed other packet files in their round while working; none opened another task's answers.
      • One run per task, on samples. 58 Godot tasks and 40 creative tasks, each run once per setup. Sol's creative lead, and Sol's and Terra's Godot lead over IQ4 and NVFP4, are statistically clear; differences among the three Qwen builds on Godot are not.
      • Exclusions. Seven GameDevBench tasks have reference solutions that fail in this arm64 VM (0012, 0015, 0051, 0052, 0059, 0068, 0130). Two fell in the sample and are left out for every run.
      • Rule breaks. On task 1255 (Mapbox in React) every Qwen build and Terra load scripts from a CDN although the prompt asked for everything inline; Sol fetches them only after the user enters a Mapbox token. On task 1568 the IQ4, IQ3 and NVFP4 pages also fetch a 3D library from a CDN, and for IQ3 and NVFP4 it fails to load. All were judged as they rendered.
      • A first creative attempt was discarded. It ran outside the sandbox, where Codex used the VM's Playwright install to check its own output.
